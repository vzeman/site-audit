Header structure (H1–H6)

How well the document outline reflects the page's content. We check three things: (1) structural rules — exactly one H1 per page, no skipped levels (H1 → H3 is a skip), every long page should have H2/H3 sub-sections; (2) title ↔ H1 alignment — when these disagree, users get a different headline from each; (3) header ↔ content alignment — every header is embedded and compared to its host page's paragraph centroid; cosine ≥ 0.65 means the header describes what's actually under it, lower is a click-bait header or a stray template fragment.

Linkbuilding overview

How the site distributes internal and external link equity. Internal:external ratio shows whether the site mostly talks to itself (high ratio, common for content-heavy sites) or sends traffic away (low ratio). Descriptive anchor share ≥ 60% is healthy — anchors that name the destination's topic. Empty links (no text, no title, no aria-label, no image alt) are accessibility failures and waste link equity. Image-only links without alt text are double-broken: useless to assistive tech and to search engines.

Paragraph link density

internal + external <a href> per 100 words, per paragraph

Healthy range: roughly 0.5–3 links per 100 words. A median of 0 with a high "zero-link share" means most paragraphs are unlinked running text — opportunities to add internal links. The spammy threshold (≥ 5 links/100w, dashed red line on the chart) flags link-stuffed paragraphs that look low-quality to LLMs and degrade page-level credibility. Saturated paragraphs are automatically excluded from the in-paragraph link recommendations below (we won't suggest more links to a paragraph that already has too many).

In-paragraph link recommendations

Closes the gap that page-level link recs leave open: which paragraph on the source page should host the link, and which exact phrase should be the anchor. The lift metric is the bar — we require the paragraph to be more relevant to the target than the source page as a whole, so the link reads as natural editorial linking rather than boilerplate. Lift ≥ 0.15 is high-confidence; lower values still help but are more arguable. Already-saturated paragraphs (≥ 5 links per 100w) are skipped automatically.

Paragraph cluster relationship views

Alternate projections of the same paragraph-cluster relationships: network neighborhoods, hierarchy, centroid map, chord bundles, and section-to-topic flow.

Network graph

Each bubble is a paragraph cluster. Bubble size = paragraph count, color = cohesion, and links connect only strongly similar clusters. Dense neighborhoods are related topic families; isolated nodes are distinct themes.

Cluster dendrogram

A hierarchy built from paragraph-cluster similarity. Clusters that join near each other are semantically closer; long branches indicate topics that stay distinct until late in the merge tree.

Centroid map

One dot per paragraph cluster, positioned from the average coordinates of its sampled paragraphs in the paragraph scatter map. Dot size = paragraph count. Nearby dots are topics that sit close in the semantic space.

Chord diagram

Outer arcs are the largest paragraph clusters. Ribbons connect clusters whose similarity clears the strong-overlap threshold. Thick bundles mean repeated semantic overlap between major topics.

HITS authorities

Linked from many hub pages — your "topic destinations." Where readers (and crawlers) end up when they're looking for a definitive answer on a sub-topic. Authorities are often different pages from the PageRank top list: PageRank is global, HITS is topical. The pages here are the canonical URLs you want LLM answer engines to cite.

HITS hubs

Pages that link out to many authorities — your "topic indexes." Hubs are usually section landing pages, blog category pages, or hand-curated guides. If a topic has no hub, an LLM has to discover the authorities on its own; a strong hub is a shortcut.

Buried pages (click depth ≥ 4 from homepage)

Pages that take 4+ clicks to reach from the homepage via the shortest internal-link path. Search engines de-prioritise deep pages, and users almost never reach them. Bring high-PageRank buried pages within 3 clicks by linking them from a section landing page or sidebar; consider redirecting low-value ones.

Most-cited external domains

Where your authors look for sources. Authoritative-TLD domains (.gov, .edu, Wikipedia, NIH, major journals) are flagged — these are the citations LLM answer engines weight most. If your top citations are mostly social media (twitter/facebook/youtube) or your own subdomains, you're missing the credibility signal that puts a page in the cite-able tier.

Citation density

External links per 1 000 words. Healthy: 5–25 / 1 000 words for content pages. Pages with zero outbound citations look unsourced to LLM answer engines — they may be factual but won't be picked as citations. Add 2–3 links to authoritative sources on every long-form page. Citation density above 100 / 1 000 words usually means a list-of-links page, not real content.
